Fairness Across Traditions
My working definition

What is fair?

Fairness is taking no advantage at the expense of any affected being1 — or of the living systems all beings depend on2 — that they cannot or could not have taken in return; making no distinction between beings that you would reject for yourself3; and where such advantage has already been taken, restoring what could not be defended4.

1

The reciprocity test

An advantage is unfair if the other side could never take it back.

Fairness in Asian thought

Three steps for a newcomer. First the big idea that sets Asian ethics apart, then the five traditions behind it, then how each region compares with the West.

1

The big idea: from "me" to "between us"

So the fairness question changes from "Was I treated equally?" to "Was this relationship kept in balance?"

A broad tendency, not a rule. Based on Confucian role ethics (R. Ames, Confucian Role Ethics, 2011) and Watsuji's "betweenness" (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). Western thought also has relational strands, such as care ethics.

Open source runs banks, hospitals and power grids. AI now finds its weak spots at scale. Who controls the defence?

The point: coordination is needed, but centralising it is the flaw. Splitting into rival regional systems would be just as weak.

Three asks

1A multi-stakeholder charter with federated nodes, not one repository.
2A binding non-discrimination rule, so no single national order can switch off defence.
3Keep unpublished vulnerabilities on neutral ground, governed by many countries.
Through my fairness definition
  • Reciprocity: allies were cut off and could not cut back. That is an advantage they cannot take in return.
  • Self-test: would the US accept another country switching off its defences?
  • Restoration: federated, neutral custody gives back what single-country control took away.
  • East–West link: federation treats security as interdependence, close to the relational view in Part 2.

Why it matters for fairness

  • Dual use: the same tool defends or attacks.
  • Asymmetry: big firms get protected first. Who can't "take it in return"?
  • Legitimacy: should a company decide what is too dangerous to share?
  • Jurisdiction: one government switched off every foreign user in hours. See Prof. Maracke's reading in Part 3.
